One-paragraph summary. No single method from the literature will close the gap to the top 10 on its own. The evidence (our learning curve, our error breakdown and the public write-ups) points to two levers. The first is more real training data: we now have the 2025 edition's 10,000 series, already wired into our code. The second is a better model of how break-free series behave, because break-free series drift as much as a typical break. The two new models in Section 7 target the second lever. Model A makes a volatility model fitted on the history the yardstick for the online data. Model B is a learned network that compares the recent data with the series' own history.

1. The problem in plain words BASIC

Each series is a sequence of numbers, for example the daily returns of an asset. It comes in two parts:

  • History (1,000 to 5,000 points): given all at once, and guaranteed to have no break.
  • Online segment (10 to 999 points): revealed one point at a time. In half of the series, the way the numbers are generated changes somewhere in this part. That change is the structural break.

After every new point we must output a score: "how likely is it that the break has already happened?"

How we are scored

At each online step (step 1, step 2, …) the platform takes all series that are still running and asks: do the series that already had their break get higher scores than those that did not? That is an AUC at each step (0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect). These are averaged over steps, with most weight on steps 100 to 700. The metric is called TS-AUC (time-stratified AUC). Two consequences:

  • Only the ranking between series at the same step matters. The absolute score value does not.
  • A score must be comparable across very different series: calm ones, wild ones, trending ones.

Why it is hard

  • The breaks are subtle. Even if you knew exactly where the break was and looked at 300 points after it, the best single simple statistic separates break from no-break series only about 60% of the time.
  • Normal series drift on their own. Their variance can swing by 50% with no break at all, which looks just like a break.
  • About 30% of breaks change nothing measurable (finding of another competitor; our own check found 4.4% that change none of five simple properties). Nobody can detect those, so no score reaches 1.0.

2. How a break detector works: from one number to a learned model BASIC → INTERMEDIATE

Every method in the field is a variation of one idea: compare the recent data with the history and measure how surprising the difference is. The methods differ in what they compare, how they turn it into a number, and who decides the rule: a statistician or a learning algorithm.

Step up the ladderIdea in plain wordsExample
1. A summary numberCompute something like the variance of the last 100 points and compare it with the history's variance."Recent variance is 1.6× the history's"
2. Accumulating evidenceKeep a running total of "surprise" that grows while the data looks unusual and resets towards zero when it looks normal. It catches small but persistent changes.CUSUM, Shiryaev-Roberts
3. Scanning all start timesWe do not know when the break began, so try every possible start and keep the most surprising one.GLR scan statistic
4. Many alternatives at onceA break could change the variance, the tails, the dependence and more. Test each kind and combine them with probabilities.Bayesian model averaging
5. A learned combinerCompute hundreds of such statistics and let a machine-learning model learn how to weigh them from labelled examples.Gradient-boosted trees (our XGBoost)
6. A learned detectorFeed (lightly processed) raw data to a neural network and let it learn its own statistics.Our GRU; TCNs; transformers

Our current system already uses every rung of this ladder. That is why "another statistic" or "another model of the same kind" now gives only tiny gains. The research question becomes: what information or structure is still missing?

4. The research field: six families of methods INTERMEDIATE → ADVANCED

A deep search covered about 20 primary sources from 2019 to 2026 (papers, official rules, public competition write-ups). Every literature claim was checked by three independent verifiers. Below, each family gets an explanation, its evidence and a verdict for this competition.

4.1 Classical sequential detection (CUSUM, Shiryaev-Roberts, GLR, BOCPD)

Idea. Assume a model for "before" and a model for "after", and accumulate the log-likelihood ratio point by point. CUSUM takes the maximum over start times, Shiryaev-Roberts the sum. Bayesian online change-point detection (BOCPD) keeps a probability for every possible "time since the break".

Status here. This is what our features already are. Conformal martingales and "e-detectors", the newer assumption-free versions, turn out to be essentially our CUSUM/SR on ECDF ranks (verified). Verdict: already exhausted.

4.2 Kernel two-sample tests (MMD, Scan-B, kernel CUSUM, random-Fourier-feature MMD)

Idea in plain words. Instead of comparing a few summary numbers (mean, variance…), compare the whole distribution of the recent points with that of the history. MMD (maximum mean discrepancy) measures the distance between two clouds of points through a similarity "kernel". It sees changes of shape that moments miss.

Evidence (verified). When the distribution changes from Gaussian to Laplace with the same mean and variance, a kernel scan detected it with power 0.44, against 0.03 to 0.04 for mean and Gaussian tests and 0.24 for the best moment-based CUSUM (i.i.d. simulation). A 2025 method (random-Fourier-feature MMD) does this online for only O(r log n) work per step by testing split points at powers of two.

Caveats. The theory assumes independent data, which our autocorrelated series are not. Applied to single values it only sees the marginal distribution: to see dependence it must be run on pairs (xt, xt−1) or differences. Its null variance differs from series to series, which is fatal for a cross-series ranking unless each series is calibrated on its own history. Verdict: promising for shape/dependence breaks, probably +0.003 to +0.005. It is the base of Model B.

4.3 Learned test statistics and density-ratio estimation

Idea. Instead of choosing the statistic by hand, train a neural network to tell "history" from "recent" data. If it can, there was a change. The classifier's output estimates the log density ratio, the ideal ingredient for CUSUM. Variants: NN-CUSUM, ONNC/ONNR, deep-kernel MMD trained to maximise test power, and RuLSIF/KLIEP (classical density-ratio estimators).

Evidence (verified). A 2024 discussion paper (Li, Fearnhead, Fryzlewicz & Wang) proves that CUSUM is exactly a small ReLU network, so learned statistics contain the classical ones. In experiments they beat CUSUM under autocorrelated or heavy-tailed noise. They beat classical density ratios on covariance changes (F1 0.93 vs 0.85) but only tie on pure variance changes (0.97 vs 0.97). The guarantees assume training and test data come from the same distribution, which matches our bad experience with synthetic breaks.

Verdict: a structurally new angle if the network is built to compare against the series' own history. This is Model B.

4.4 Time-series foundation models (Chronos, TimesFM, Moirai, MOMENT)

Idea. Large pretrained forecasting models. Either use their forecast errors (a break makes forecasts worse) or their internal embeddings as features.

Evidence (weak). Two unreviewed 2026 preprints with split verifier votes. Embeddings helped on weak shifts in toy AR(1) windows but lost to simple statistics on strong ones. Forecast errors spike at a break, then fall back once the model adapts in-context, so they must be accumulated. One competitor gained +0.004 on the board from Chronos-2 features, but that part needs a GPU when the model runs on the platform. Verdict: expensive, small, risky under the platform's CPU/time limits.

4.5 Multiscale, spectral, signatures, state-space models, simulation-trained detectors

The search found no verified evidence for wavelets, path signatures, Mamba/S4 or detectors trained on simulated priors (PFN-style) for this kind of task. Our own synthetic-break experiment suggests simulation-trained detectors would suffer the same mismatch. Verdict: no evidence; low priority.

4.6 Normal-behaviour models (model the "no break" world very well)

Idea. Instead of modelling breaks, model the normal process precisely and flag deviations. Forecasting nets (NLinear, GRU) trained on histories, quantile networks giving the probability of each new point (PIT), and volatility models such as GARCH.

Evidence. One competitor gained +0.005 on the board from NLinear/GRU normal-behaviour models. Another gained +0.013 on the board from a stream whitened by a "conditional scale" (read directly from their write-up). Our own analysis (Section 6) shows that normal drift is the main obstacle. Verdict: most promising family for us. This is Model A.

6. What our own data says INTERMEDIATE

We split the best blend's performance by the kind of change each break actually made (measured after the fact).

Reading. Breaks that raise the variance are caught well. The rest are barely caught. A check showed this is not a model failure: in break series, falls in variance are no more common than in break-free series (9 to 11% of break series fall below the break-free 10th percentile, which is chance). Breaks in this data mostly raise variance, and the other kinds of break are simply very faint.

The real obstacle: break-free series drift

  • Over 100 points, the variance of a break-free series swings with a standard deviation of 0.41 in log units, about ±50%. That is as large as a typical break.
  • This drift is partly predictable from the end of the history: the correlation with the variance of the last 200 history points is about 0.5. Calm histories stay calm for a while, and wild ones stay wild. This is volatility clustering.
  • Comparing online variance against the end of the history instead of its average improved a single variance statistic by +0.015 to +0.03 (AUC, measured on early breaks, so the gain at steps 100 to 700 is probably smaller).
  • Our tree features compare everything with whole-history constants. The GRU sees only the last 100 history points.

7. Two new model ideas ADVANCED

Both ideas change what the model compares against, the lever the evidence points to, instead of adding another model of the same kind. Each can be tested in a day or two with our existing CV machinery.

Model A: "Volatility-anchored detector" (a normal-behaviour model of the variance path)

The intuition. Today we ask "is the recent variance unusual compared with the history on average?". But a series whose history ended in a calm spell will naturally drift back up, and that looks like a break. Model A asks the better question: "is the recent variance unusual compared with what this series' own volatility dynamics predicted?"

How it works.

  1. Fit a volatility model on the history only. Use GARCH(1,1) with Student-t errors on the AR(10) residuals et we already compute: σ²t = ω + α·e²t−1 + β·σ²t−1. Three numbers per series. It can be fitted by a short deterministic optimisation, or by a fixed grid over (α, β) with ω set by the long-run variance, which is fast in numba.
  2. Frozen forecast path (the "anchor"). From the last history point, the model predicts the variance k steps ahead: σ̂²(k) = σ̄² + (α+β)k·(σ²T − σ̄²). It starts at the end-of-history level and decays to the long-run level. It is never updated with online data, so it cannot absorb a break.
  3. Anchored stream. ut = et / σ̂(t). Under "no break", u is close to standard. After a variance break it is not. Run our existing machinery (CUSUM, Shiryaev-Roberts, scans, tail counts) on u, u² and log|u| instead of on e.
  4. Second stream: online-updated GARCH. Keep updating σ²t online with the history's parameters and compute the standardized residual zt = et/σt. After a break, the GARCH filter misfits: its forecast errors of e² and the tail frequency of z shift, and its dependence signature changes. This is close to the "conditional scale whitening" that gave another team +0.013 on the board.
  5. Feed both. Add the anchored statistics as about 20 to 30 new tree features, and ut, u²t−1 and zt as three new GRU channels. Optionally train a separate member on the anchored stream only and blend it in.
Why it should helpRisks
Targets the measured main obstacle (normal drift). Uses volatility clustering, which is strong in this data. Backed by our oracle test (+0.015 to +0.03) and a competitor's board gain (+0.013). Cheap: O(1) per point. GARCH may fit poorly on near-unit-root or very heavy-tailed series (use robust starting values and clip α+β ≤ 0.995). The gain may mostly duplicate what the GRU already learns from its 100-point warm-up, so the paired test decides.

First experiment (1 day). (1) Oracle check: rerun diagnostics/null_spread.py with the GARCH anchor at all break positions. (2) Add the three GRU channels and run both seeds against oof_gru_aug2(_s1). (3) Add the kernel increments and run the trees against oof_aug2_evc. Keep it only if the usual rule passes: paired ≥ +0.003, above 2 standard errors, at least 4 of 5 folds.

Model B: "History-vs-recent comparator network" (a learned two-sample test)

The intuition. Our GRU reads the series from left to right and must discover by itself that its job is to compare the recent data with the past. Model B builds that comparison into the architecture. A small network learns a "description" of short patches of data. The model then measures how far the description of recent patches has moved from the description of the history's patches. This is the learned version of the MMD kernel test (Section 4.2) and of the classifier two-sample tests (Section 4.3).

How it works.

  1. Patch encoder φ. A tiny 1-D CNN or MLP (about 5k parameters) maps a patch of 16 to 32 consecutive whitened residuals (plus differences, products etet−1 and |e|) to a vector of 16 to 32 numbers. The same φ is shared by all series.
  2. History reference. Apply φ to all history patches once and store their mean μH and standard deviation sH per dimension. This also gives a per-series null: how much φ naturally varies within this series, which is exactly the calibration that kernel tests lack.
  3. Recent summaries. Keep running means of φ over the last 32, 64, 128, 256 and 512 online points (dyadic suffixes, as in the 2025 RFF-MMD paper), plus "everything since step 0".
  4. Standardised gaps. gs = (means − μH) / (sH/√ns) for each scale s. Clipped, these feed a small head (MLP or GRU over steps) that outputs the score. The max over scales plays the role of the scan over start times.
  5. Training. End to end with our metric-matched weighted cross-entropy, on the Real-Time series, the re-cut variants and the 2025 series. A learned φ can discover the shape and dependence changes our hand-made statistics miss. The architecture guarantees the right comparison, so it needs far less data than a free-form GRU.
  6. Non-learned warm-up version (cheap first step). Replace φ with random Fourier features of (et, et−1) and |e|-pairs. That gives online RFF-MMD features calibrated per series, which can be added to the trees in a few hours.
Why it should helpRisks
Structurally different from both current models, so it should blend well. Self-calibrates per series. Theory says such statistics contain CUSUM as a special case. The literature shows gains where dependence or heavy tails are involved, which are exactly our weak slices. Inference is O(1) per point per scale. The verified gains are on dependence/covariance changes, not pure variance changes (there it only ties), so it must complement the trees. Training needs care (the history reference must use only history patches; patches must not straddle the boundary).

First experiment (2 days). (1) The RFF-MMD feature version for the trees, paired against oof_aug2_evc. (2) The learned version as a third blend member, both seeds, then blend.py with all three.

9. Glossary and sources

TermMeaning
AUC / TS-AUCProbability that a random break series scores above a random no-break series. TS-AUC averages it per online step.
CUSUM / Shiryaev-RobertsRunning totals of evidence for a change. CUSUM keeps the best start time, SR sums over all start times.
GLR scanTest every possible break start and keep the strongest evidence.
WhiteningTransforming the data so that, under "no break", it looks like independent standard noise (here: AR residuals, ECDF ranks).
Volatility clustering / GARCHLarge moves follow large moves. GARCH predicts tomorrow's variance from today's.
MMDMaximum mean discrepancy: a distance between two distributions through a kernel. Zero only if they are equal.
Density ratioHow much more likely a point is under "after" than under "before". A classifier separating the two estimates it.
Re-cut augmentationNew training series made by moving the history/online boundary of real ones. The labels stay exact.
Paired comparisonThe same folds scored with and without a change. It removes the fold-to-fold noise.
